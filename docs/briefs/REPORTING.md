# ERC Research Brief: Reporting Results

DRAFT for Kate's rulings, Oct 22, 2026.

This file covers how a brief reports a paper's results: which numbers go in, in what form, and where. Two other files cover the rest, and this one does not repeat them:

- `STYLE-GUIDE.md`: voice, causal verbs, hedging, verbatim numbers with context, and visuals that earn their place.
- `STANDARDIZATION.md`, "Figures & tables": the look of every table and figure.

Where this file touches either one, it says so.

**Where the rules come from.** Eight published briefs, each read against its paper in October 2026: bowen, cortes, holzman (key holzman-emerge), kwok, meer, nagro, svajda-hardy and williams. Three readings had limits: kwok's paper came through a summary that left out the bodies of its Tables 1 and 2; svajda-hardy's through its AERA 2025 conference version only; meer's through NBER WP 33676, not the journal text the brief cites. cortes was read against its journal article in full and pages 1 to 46 of the 57-page working paper. Each rule gives its count. The count is out of the briefs the rule could apply to: a brief with no statistical tests cannot follow a rule about stars. **Thin** marks a rule with only one or two briefs behind it. Follow a thin rule as a default, not a law.

## 1. Significance

**No stars, no p-values, no test statistics, anywhere.** (5 of 5 briefs with tests: bowen, cortes, holzman, kwok, meer.) Not in a table, a chart, a note or the prose.
*Example:* the paper's "p ≤ 0.001" became "a statistically significant difference" (kwok).

**Say significance in words.** (3 of 5: bowen, kwok, meer.) holzman never says it. cortes turns its nulls into words instead ("experience no loss" for 0.005 (0.005), "no net loss"), the hiding question 3 warns against. Write "statistically significant" for a result that clears the bar. For one that does not, write "not statistically significant" or "not statistically distinguishable from zero". Keep "significant" for the statistical sense: bowen's Conclusions say "made a significant difference" in the everyday sense.
*Example:* "small and not statistically significant" (meer).

**A null difference is not "equal", and a group with a non-significant estimate is not counted in a claimed gap.** cortes's Highlight 03 says "equal sensitivity" where the paper says "no statistically significant differences". Its Executive Summary ties Black students to widening degree gaps on an estimate of -0.009 (0.009).

**In a table of estimates, significance gets its own column of one-word chips.** (Thin: bowen, meer.) In both, each chip has one of two values and the word always comes with its colour; that describes them, it is not a rule (question 2 adds a third value). Which words and colours to use, and where to draw the line, are open (questions 1 and 2).
*Example:* "First-generation students | 35.6 percentage points | Yes" (meer).

## 2. Uncertainty

**No standard errors, confidence intervals or error bars.** (3 of 4 briefs with estimates: bowen, holzman, meer.) bowen drew a chart with 95% ranges and chose a table without them instead. cortes's Figure 1 draws capped whiskers, the paper's 95% confidence intervals, unlabeled. Whiskers, if drawn, are labeled as what they are.

**Where the paper stresses imprecision, the prose says so in words.** (Thin: meer.)
*Example:* "noisy because these outcomes are rare" (meer).

## 3. Units

**A change in a share is "percentage points", spelled out, in prose and in table cells.** (3 of 3 causal briefs: bowen, holzman, meer.) Never write "percent", even where the paper does: bowen rewrote the paper's "3 percent less likely" as "3.0 percentage points lower". The readers found "pp" once in eight briefs, on meer's chart; STYLE-GUIDE §5 uses it in its group-gap example (question 16).
*Example:* "Disciplinary infractions | 3.0 percentage points lower" (bowen).

**As an adjective it takes no hyphen.** (Thin: bowen, meer. cortes is inconsistent.) "a 2.7 percentage point increase" (bowen), "a 26.5 percentage point increase" (meer). cortes mixes "5.4 percentage point drop", "4–6 percentage-point drop", "3.7 percentage-point" and a bare "points".

**A coefficient on a yes/no outcome is multiplied by 100 to give percentage points.** (4 of 4 briefs with estimates.)
*Example:* 0.088 became "8.8 percentage points" (meer).

**The visual and the prose use the same unit, and the axis names it.** cortes plots proportions to two decimals on a y axis with "no title and no unit (-0.04 means 4 percentage points)", while its prose gives percentage points to one decimal.

**Levels and shares take the % symbol.** (6 of 7 briefs that print a share.) This is STYLE-GUIDE §3's rule. The exception is STYLE-GUIDE's own anchor, cortes, which breaks it: its cover writes "21.5% of the mean", its body "Roughly 9 percent" and "93 percent".
*Example:* "a baseline rate of 21.7%" (meer).

**Relative change is split.** A multiple in words (bowen "1.5 times more likely", holzman "nearly tripling the rate", kwok "approximately twice the 57-word average") or the % symbol (meer, "an increase of roughly 40%").

**An effect in standard deviations keeps its unit in words, at two decimals.** (Thin: bowen alone.)
*Example:* "0.24 standard deviations higher".

## 4. Decimals, rounding and number form

**Print numbers at the precision the paper printed.** (kwok, meer, nagro.) STYLE-GUIDE §3 asks for verbatim numbers, but verbatim copies a paper's own error (question 14). The briefs keep them even when one sentence ends up mixing decimals: "approximately 33.1% in 1990 to 64% in fall 2018" (nagro). The exceptions: holzman rounded its effects to whole points, bowen rounded 0.242 to "0.24" and 5.03 and 9.86 to "about five" and "nearly ten", and meer's prose says women gained "about 8" where its table says 8.1 (questions 5 and 12).

**In prose, a rounded count or context figure usually says so:** "about", "roughly", "nearly" (kwok "nearly 1,900" for 1,894, williams "about 100", svajda-hardy "About 81%", cortes "Roughly 9 percent" for 9.4%). Some shares go unhedged: williams' "(2%)" for 87 of 3,988, cortes's "93 percent" for 0.926. Rounded effect estimates mostly go unhedged: holzman's 0.1748 as "17 percentage points", bowen's 0.242 as "0.24", cortes's -0.042 to -0.059 as "4–6"; meer's "about 8" for 8.1 is the exception. In a table, only svajda-hardy marks rounding, with "~" (question 9).

**Thousands take a comma.** (7 of 7.) "10,600 students", "5,207 pages".

**Value ranges are open (question 15).** bowen, nagro and williams write "to" ("9.7% to 13.9%"), and nagro changed the paper's "6–21" to "6 to 21". cortes writes "4–6 percentage-point drop" in Finding 1 and "about 3 to 4" and "roughly 2 to 3" in Findings 2 and 3. Year spans keep the en dash (Thin: kwok, williams, "2020–2021"), and so do page ranges in references.

**Small counts of the study's own parts are words; data are digits.** (Thin: kwok, svajda-hardy.) "three levels", "Five states", but "Houston (9) or Dallas (5)" (williams). nagro writes its 12 leaders both as "12" and as "twelve".

## 5. Signs and direction

**Prose never prints a sign. Direction is a word.** (4 of 4 briefs with estimates: bowen, cortes, holzman, meer.) "lower", "higher", "increased by", "more likely", "less often".
*Example:* "8.8 percentage points more likely" (meer).

**A negative estimate is printed as its size plus "lower" or "fewer". It is never replaced by words**, a significant harm included. So far the briefs hid them: bowen's reading, math and science (-0.040, -0.057, -0.056) and meer's earnings (-$179) became words (question 3). The ban on a minus sign holds for prose only: cortes's Figure 1 plots negative coefficients on ticks from -0.02 to -0.06. Tables split three ways on signs (question 4).

## 6. Sample size

**The sample size is in the prose.** (8 of 8.)
*Example:* "more than 1.5 million" (cortes).

**When the estimate rests on a much smaller sample than the study's pool, the prose gives the estimate's sample, and a small subgroup gets its N.** No brief does this yet. holzman prints "1,078 students who applied to EMERGE in fall 2016" for an estimate on about 520 of them. meer's "35.6 percentage points" for first-generation students rests on 123 students, undisclosed, beside the 8.8 for all 1,748. No table or chart of estimates shows an N (3 of 3: bowen, holzman, meer).

**A descriptive visual names its base in the header or caption.** (Thin: svajda-hardy, williams.) "Share of 31 CBM statutes", "(33 schools)".

**A small share comes with its count.** (4 of 6 briefs that print shares: kwok, nagro, svajda-hardy, williams.) Either can come first. Two briefs also restate a share as "1 in N": kwok "Fewer than 1 in 25 responses (3.8%)", nagro "Roughly 1 in 7".
*Example:* "Only 75 responses, or 3.8%, met this criterion" (kwok).

## 7. Titles and labels

**A table's caption is a plain label. The finding is the heading above it.** (6 of 6 tables, in bowen, meer, nagro and svajda-hardy.) bowen's draft title "What changed when schools added arts learning." became "Effects of expanded arts learning on behavior, attendance, and test scores."; meer's drafts "Who responded most" and "What the email changed" became labels. No published table carries a caption sentence or a "What it shows" box: the drafted captions were cut (meer's both, with "(Baseline rate of taking the next course: 21.7%.)", svajda-hardy's both, bowen's "plain-language caption").
*Example:* "Effect of email on course enrollment, by student group" (meer).

**A chart of results gets a finding sentence; a diagram gets a label.** (4 of 4 result charts: cortes, holzman, meer, williams. 2 of 2 diagrams: kwok, svajda-hardy, whose review versions both carried finding sentences that became labels.) The title claims only what the visual shows, at the paper's strength. williams' "DEEP schools cluster near the Texas-Mexico border and around Houston and Dallas" overstates the paper, and its own prose says "Only 14 of the 87 schools were in Houston (9) or Dallas (5)".
*Examples:* "The email cutoff produced a sharp jump in next-course enrollment." (meer); "The three escalating levels of equity-oriented teaching" (kwok).

**Rows and columns are plain words in sentence case, never variable names.** (5 of 6 tables.) In meer, "Female" became "Women" and "Switches Any Major" became "Switches major (any field)". The exceptions: svajda-hardy Table 1 keeps the paper's coding names (Audience, Provider, Setting) glossed in a plain-words column, and leaves CBM, PD and "classroom mgmt" unexpanded. Reused or copied chart labels: holzman and williams reuse the paper's images and with them its labels ("Mean of Enrollment in MC Colleges"); cortes's legends keep "Non-Dis. / Econ. Dis." and its Panel A is titled "By URM Status" over race groups.

**In an effects table the unit sits in every cell, not in the header.** (Thin: bowen, meer.) A shares table puts it in the header (svajda-hardy).

**The prose cites each visual by number, as STYLE-GUIDE §5 requires.** (3 of 7 recorded: bowen "(Table 1)", kwok "(Figure 1)", nagro "shown in Table 1", each a jump link. cortes, holzman, meer and williams never do; svajda-hardy is unrecorded.)

**Alt-text numbers and units match the visual, and the alt text names the kind of visual.** Three briefs restate the visual's numbers in alt text (meer, svajda-hardy, williams). meer's says "class rank" for a standardized score. cortes's says "Line chart showing degree completion over time" for a plot of coefficients with whiskers.

## 8. The note line

There is no rule yet. Two of the eight briefs print a note under a visual: nagro ("*Note.* Adapted from Nagro et al. (2026), Table 3.") and kwok ("Note." plus a sentence, then "Source: Kwok et al. (2026)."). The other six print none, among them meer, the layout source of truth, bowen, the newest, and cortes. STANDARDIZATION.md sets how a note looks, and for one component when: the levels figure's anatomy moves "The source `.table-note` ... INSIDE the figure-card". For the rest it is silent. See question 6.

## 9. Chart or table

- **Several outcomes or groups: a small table.** (Thin: bowen, meer.) One table can hold outcomes in mixed units, with group rows separating them (bowen: "Behavior and attendance" in percentage points, "State test scores" in standard deviations). bowen drew bar and dot charts and chose the table.
- **The headline effect of a cutoff design: the cutoff (RD) plot**, with a caption that says how to read the jump. (Thin: holzman and meer, the only two RD briefs.)
- **A framework.** Levels that build on each other get the levels figure (Thin: kwok). A set of themes, each defined, gets a prose table (Thin: nagro). STANDARDIZATION.md has both components.
- **A funnel whose steps are in different units: equal boxes, each number over its unit word, never bars on one scale.** (Thin: svajda-hardy, which dropped a bar option for this reason.)
- **Shares of one base: a table with in-cell bars, sorted, with the sort named in the caption.** (Thin: svajda-hardy.)
- **Where things are: a map, which says what one mark stands for.** (Thin: williams, whose dots are cities but whose caption reads as one dot per school.)
- **One visual is the norm.** (6 of 8.) meer and svajda-hardy carry three each.
- STYLE-GUIDE §5's "actual vs. benchmark" table and its group-gap table have not been built in any brief yet; williams kept its staffing ratios in prose (question 16).

## 10. What stays in prose

- **Context for the headline effect:** a baseline, a relative change, a benchmark or a real-world unit. (3 of 3 causal briefs.) It sits in the adjacent prose or the note. No table carries it yet: bowen and meer left the baseline out of theirs, and meer's drafts carried the 21.7% baseline, then cut it. STYLE-GUIDE §3's form is "% of the mean" or a comparison. Only cortes prints "% of the mean", and only in Highlight 02; Highlight 01 and Finding 2 give none, although the paper gives 13.9% and 12.2%.
  *Example:* "0.5 percentage points, equivalent to about one additional day per school year" (bowen).
- **The main null results are reported, never dropped.** This is a rule, not yet practice: holzman dropped its null information-packet trial, meer two null major variants (0.041 and 0.033), and cortes reports its nulls only as words.
  *Example:* "no measurable difference" in SAT scores (holzman).
- **Subgroups: the ones the paper emphasizes, favourable or not.** No brief states a rule, and all three causal briefs select. bowen omits significant results such as autism discipline (-0.031***) and five attendance subgroups. holzman omits the gender, Black/Hispanic and HBCU results. meer omits paper Table 4's majoring and minoring rows. See question 8.
- **Statistics from other studies.** (bowen, holzman, nagro, williams.) None appears in a visual yet, though STYLE-GUIDE §5 would put williams' staffing ratios, an outside source, in an actual-vs-benchmark table.
- **Design checks, in words with no numbers.** (Thin: meer. bowen implies balance only through "randomized", and holzman leaves design checks out.)
  *Example:* students "did not cluster near the cutoff" (meer).
- **In a qualitative study, how often each theme came up**, in words and never counted. (Thin: nagro. kwok counts a screening code: "Only 75 responses, or 3.8%".)
  *Example:* "the most emphasized theme, appearing repeatedly for each respondent" (nagro).
- **Effect-size adjectives are split.** bowen uses none: the authors' "substantial" is not used. meer uses "large", "far larger", "meaningfully more likely" and "small". holzman's figure title dropped "substantially" between Apr 13 and May 4.

## 11. What changes from the paper's version

- **One estimate per outcome, from the model the authors prefer or emphasize.** (3 of 3 causal briefs.) The other columns, standard errors, Ns, stars and controls rows go. meer keeps the paper's reduced form and drops the IV.
- **Every label is rewritten in plain words**, with section 7's exceptions.
- **Exhibits are combined.** bowen takes behavior from one model column and test scores from another. meer's Table 1 joins rows from the paper's Tables 3 and 4. williams merges two maps into one figure. None of them says so on the page (question 6).
- **Charts are redrawn in the ERC style** (`assets/erc_dataviz.mplstyle`, STYLE-GUIDE §5), with plain axes and percents. (Thin: meer.) cortes redrew its chart in a default sans font with no maroon; holzman and williams reuse the paper's image. STANDARDIZATION.md lists all three as older briefs not yet brought up to the standard.

## Open questions for Kate

Each question lists what the briefs chose and gives a recommendation. Until you rule, a draft flags the choice for you.

1. **Chip words and colours in a significance column.** bowen uses a "Result" column with "Improved" or "No change". meer uses "Statistically significant?" with "Yes" or "No". Both reuse `.gap-pill` green `.pos` for significant and grey `.zero` for not, colours STYLE-GUIDE §5 and STANDARDIZATION define for sign or direction, so a significant harm would wear green "pos". *Recommendation:* meer's words. They name what they measure, and "Improved" has no word for a significant harm. Give significance chips colours of their own.
2. **Whether to state significance, and what to do with p < 0.10.** holzman states significance nowhere, so its p < 0.10 and p < 0.01 results read the same. Neither table names the threshold behind its chip. bowen gives a p < 0.10 result the same "Improved" chip as its p < 0.05 results and puts the caveat only in prose ("meets a less stringent standard of statistical significance"). It then calls a p < 0.10 subgroup plainly "statistically significant". *Recommendation:* always state significance in words, and let "Yes" mean p < 0.05. A p < 0.10 result reads "Yes (at the 10% level)" in a table (bowen's own first plan) and "a less stringent standard" in prose, every time. The note names the threshold.
3. **Null estimates.** bowen prints no number for its three nulls ("No meaningful change"). meer prints three of its nulls beside "No" (+3.2, +1.5, +4.6) but hides the fourth, a negative dollar figure, behind "No meaningful change". cortes prints no number for its nulls ("experience no loss", "no net loss"). *Recommendation:* print the estimate beside "No". "No meaningful change" reads as a judgment of size, and it hides a negative sign.
4. **Signs in tables.** bowen puts direction words in the cell ("3.0 percentage points lower"). meer Table 1 puts the direction in the header over a bare number ("Increase in taking the next economics course"). meer Table 2 uses a plus sign ("+8.8 percentage points"), so meer prints the same 8.8 both ways. *Recommendation:* words in the cell. The prose already does this, and it handles a table that mixes rises and falls.
5. **Decimals and hedges for percentage-point effects.** bowen, cortes and meer use one decimal; holzman uses whole points (0.1748 became "17 percentage points"). This clashes with section 4: at the paper's precision 0.1748 is 17.48, not 17.5. Only meer hedges a rounded estimate ("about 8"). *Recommendation:* one decimal, keeping the trailing zero ("3.0"), as the stated exception to section 4; a coarser rounding ("4–6") says "about".
6. **The note line.** Six briefs print none (bowen, cortes, holzman, meer, svajda-hardy, williams). nagro prints "*Note.* Adapted from Nagro et al. (2026), Table 3." kwok prints "Note." plus a sentence and "Source: Kwok et al. (2026).", although ERC wrote its descriptions. *Recommendation:* every visual built from the paper carries nagro's "Adapted from" line. The note also holds what a reader needs to read the visual correctly: the exhibits combined, the threshold behind "Yes", what whiskers show, abbreviations and a scale key. It holds no interpretive sentences. This would change six published briefs' practice.
7. **Row order.** The briefs use five orders: the paper's (nagro); by size, named in the caption (svajda-hardy Table 1, "ranked by frequency"); by size under an all-students row (meer Table 1); the prose order of Finding 3 (svajda-hardy Table 2); and with the significant result moved to the top (bowen). meer Table 2 moves Switches before Earnings with no stated logic. *Recommendation:* the paper's order, or by size with the caption saying so. Never reorder only so the significant result leads.
8. **Subgroups.** bowen (by your call of Sep 17) and holzman keep subgroups in prose; meer puts them in a table. *Recommendation:* a table only when the paper reports subgroups as a main result. Suggestive or post hoc subgroups stay in prose, at the strength the paper gives them.
9. **"~" on table shares.** svajda-hardy alone writes "~90%", for shares that are exact counts out of 31, and STANDARDIZATION's `.databar-table` pattern writes `~90%` too. *Recommendation:* drop the "~", and update STANDARDIZATION with the ruling. A whole-number share is rounded, not estimated.
10. **The effect's value on a cutoff chart.** meer prints "+ 8.8 pp" in a green bracket; holzman prints no value. *Recommendation:* print it in the prose's words ("8.8 percentage points"), in a colour that does not stand in for significance. The green is outside STYLE-GUIDE's palette (maroon, rose, blue, ochre, teal, plum) anyway.
11. **Reused paper charts.** holzman and williams keep the paper's image with its axis and legend words; cortes and meer redraw. *Recommendation:* redraw in the ERC style, STYLE-GUIDE §5's default. Until an older brief is redrawn, relabel its image in plain words.
12. **Prose and visual disagree.** meer gives women "about 8" in prose and 8.1 in the table. williams' caption says no schools are in central Texas, but its own map shows a dot at Austin. svajda-hardy's prose says Illinois "has strong teacher unions" where its table says "Very strong". *Recommendation:* make it a rule that prose and visual print the same number at the same precision, and that no sentence claims what the visual contradicts.
13. **A period after a table caption.** bowen and nagro end with one; meer and svajda-hardy do not. *Recommendation:* no period, as in meer.
14. **STYLE-GUIDE's 21.5% example.** STYLE-GUIDE §3 cites cortes's "3.7 percentage points (21.5% of the mean)". It is probably the paper's own error: 0.037/0.172, Black's mean, because the paper's Table 5 swaps the Hispanic and Black means against its Table 2, A.6 and Fig. 2. Against Hispanic's 0.198 it is 18.7%. Verbatim copies the error, and "use the number in the paper's tables" cannot settle it when the tables disagree with each other (williams has the same problem). *Recommendation:* when the paper's tables disagree, print no figure that rests on the disputed value until the author confirms it, and replace STYLE-GUIDE's example until then.
15. **Value ranges.** STYLE-GUIDE §3 says "En dashes in number/page ranges are correct and should be kept (e.g., 643–680, 163–206)". bowen, nagro and williams write "to", nagro changing the paper's "6–21" to "6 to 21"; cortes mixes both. *Recommendation:* "to" in prose, the en dash in year spans, page ranges and table cells, one form per brief; STYLE-GUIDE §3 changes with the ruling.
16. **STYLE-GUIDE §5's group-gap table.** Its example, "Hispanic 6.3pp enrollment / 3.7pp degree; lower-income 5.4 / 2.6; White ~0", mixes horizons: 6.3 is enrollment within one year, 3.7 a degree after six years. Neither the brief nor the guide says so, and its "pp" conflicts with the spelled-out rule. *Recommendation:* if a brief builds it, each column names its outcome and its horizon, in "percentage points".
17. **Causal verbs in an observational brief.** STYLE-GUIDE holds cortes up as saying "is associated with". The brief says "Distance reduces college enrollment", "drives" and "translates directly into fewer credits", beside its own "descriptive patterns, not definitive causal effects". *Recommendation:* associational verbs for an observational design, and a different STYLE-GUIDE example.

## Where a brief may deviate

A result that fits no rule is reported as it is, with the reason in the note. Never bend the data to fit a rule or to make a cleaner picture.

- **The paper's numbers conflict.** Use the number in the paper's tables, name the conflict in the note and flag it to Kate. When the tables disagree with each other, that does not settle it (question 14): williams' paper titles Table 1 N = 80 and Table 2 N = 137, and only Table 4 and the Discussion say 87, which the brief uses; cortes's Table 5 swaps two means. bowen used the tables' 1,971 over the introduction's 1,876 and kept the paper's p < 0.10 star on writing, though the standard error implies p < 0.01; you chose on 2026-09-17 not to ask Bowen about either. None of the three says so on the page.
- **A chart is rebuilt from the paper.** Plot the paper's data, or values read off its figure, and say which. Keep the data file and the script in the brief's folder. Draw only the paper's own fits. Keep every period and every point the paper uses to qualify its result; an author's request to drop one goes to Kate. Let the caption claim only what is plotted, and let the axis show where it starts. meer's redraw dropped two outer bins (its script says at the author's request), among them the 33% top bin the paper uses to question the effect; starts the y-axis at 15%, which makes the jump look larger; reads its points off the paper's image; and draws fit lines built so the gap at the cutoff is exactly 8.8 (20.6 - 12x, 29.4 - 43x). cortes's redraw does not match the paper's Fig. 9 by eye at years 2 to 5 (Hispanic year 3 about -0.035 against about -0.022), drops year 8 from Panel B, drops the Bachelor's column its caption's bachelor's claim rests on, and leaves no data file or script.
- **Prose goes past the paper.** Every number traces to the paper or a cited source, and no sentence is stronger than the paper. cortes's "85 school districts and 406 campuses" appears in neither version of the paper as read. Finding 3 says White students earn bachelor's degrees "because they transfer"; the paper says they enroll at four-year colleges from the start. Finding 2 says shortfalls "persist ... before leveling off"; the paper says the gap grows until 4 to 5 years, then flattens. The Conclusions say "primary pathway"; the paper says "an important pathway".
- **There is no number to show** (themes, a framework, an inventory). Report it in words and never invent counts (kwok, nagro).
- **The steps are in mixed units.** Keep each unit word with its number, and never put the steps on one scale (svajda-hardy).
- **The paper runs no tests.** Leave significance out, and do not borrow statistical words such as "associations" for a study that measured none (nagro).
- **The paper's scale has ends.** Keep them, with the key in the note. svajda-hardy's "Weak" stands for the paper's weakest union tier, so a reader cannot tell it is the end of the scale.
- **Robustness and subgroups.** STYLE-GUIDE §3 says the brief keeps the paper's caution, but two causal briefs went past it here. meer called a bandwidth-sensitive result "robust" where the paper calls it "suggestive". holzman turned a post hoc subgroup result into "the strongest effects". Report both at the strength the paper gives them.

## Not covered yet

No brief has yet reported any of these: a difference-in-differences or event study; an odds ratio or another non-linear estimate; a dollar or percent-change outcome (meer's one dollar result was hidden); a descriptive gap between groups in a visual; a significant harm; or a p < 0.10 result in a Yes/No column. An observational regression estimate and a trend over time each have one brief, cortes: thin, and not a model to copy (questions 14 and 17, and its rebuilt chart above). Follow the nearest rule, say in the note what was done, and flag it to Kate.

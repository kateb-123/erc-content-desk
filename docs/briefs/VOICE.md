# VOICE.md: the ERC research brief voice

DRAFT for Kate's rulings, Oct 22, 2026. Read from the eight published briefs, each beside its paper and, where one exists, its first draft.

The models are the four briefs Kate likes: **Bowen** (Making Room for the Arts), **Meer** (The Economics of Encouragement), **Cortes** (Distance to Degrees) and **Holzman** (EMERGE). **Nagro** (More Than Coursework) is the counter-example, quoted only for what not to do (Kate, Oct 22: "nagros was shit"). Kwok, Svajda-Hardy and Williams are not weighted. A rule that only Bowen of the four follows is marked **(Bowen's direction)**. Bowen's prose was fixed on Sep 17 and went out Oct 19.

## 1. The voice

*Approved by Kate, Oct 22.*

An ERC brief reads like a neutral report for a policymaker's staff, not an argument. It says what the study found in plain past-tense sentences and backs each claim with a number and its context where the paper gives one. It names the design once in plain words, and the design licenses causal verbs for the main effects only; nothing is ever firmer than the paper's own wording. The authors are credited for interpretations, and the brief never says "we". Its body is formal but not academic: no acronyms for the reader to carry, no praise words, no idioms. The highlights and the close may carry some energy, the way Cortes and Holzman do, never past what the paper found (Kate, Oct 22). It is short: three highlights, a few findings, a few implications, a plain close.

## 2. Section by section

*Approved by Kate, Oct 22, with her rulings on the policy layout, caveats and the close.*

**Title and cover.** A short hook title over a conventional subtitle in title case, and no question. When torn, take the conventional subtitle (for Bowen, Kate picked the plainest of seven). The cover lists the study authors and their affiliations; the brief author goes on the back page.
- "Making Room for the Arts" / "The Impact of Arts Learning on Students With Disabilities"
- "Distance to Degrees" / "How Proximity Shapes Opportunity in Texas" (Cortes)

**Executive summary.** Two paragraphs with no effect sizes. The first gives the policy context and ends on the gap. The second names the journal, says what the study evaluated, gives the design and the sample in short separate sentences, then states the results.
- "Community colleges are a key pathway to higher education, but many students live too far to attend." (Cortes)
- "Schools were selected for the Houston Arts Access Initiative by lottery. The study followed 1,971 students with disabilities in 42 elementary and middle schools." (Bowen)

**Report highlights.** Three, numbered 01 to 03. Each headline is a bold past-tense sentence with no numbers; one or two sentences under it carry the numbers. All three are this study's findings. A headline may carry some punch (Cortes, Holzman) as long as it says only what the study found. A subgroup's hedge goes in its headline **(Bowen's direction)**.
- "Certain student groups appeared to benefit more." (Bowen)
- "These gaps are not explained by academic preparation." (Cortes)
- "EMERGE changed students' college trajectories without changing their test scores." (Holzman)

**Introduction.** Two or three paragraphs: a national number with an endnote, the access or preparation gap, the evidence gap, then one sentence on what the brief does.
- "However, most existing evidence comes from small pilot studies rather than rigorous experiments." (Bowen)
- "This brief reviews the first large-scale experiment on the effects of expanded arts learning for students with disabilities." (Bowen)

**Research overview.** Three or four paragraphs: the data and sample (shares in parentheses), the program or setting, the design (what the researchers did, then its name and what it allows), the limitations under a set opener; this order is **(Bowen's direction)**. Each limitation is one sentence that gives its consequence. Keep how the data were collected in one clause.
- "Demand for the initiative exceeded capacity in its first two years, so participation was determined by lottery." (Bowen)
- "The authors note several limitations." (Bowen)
- "the results are best understood as descriptive patterns, not definitive causal effects." (Cortes)

**Key findings.** A "Finding N" label, a bold declarative past-tense headline, and one paragraph: the main effect with its number and the table pointer, then a baseline or a benchmark, then subgroups, then the authors' interpretation last. A null result may be framed as the answer to a decision-maker's worry, only where the paper says it found no evidence, and keeping its precision caveat ("cannot rule out modest effects", Meer) **(Bowen's direction)**.
- "Writing scores rose." (Bowen)
- "A common concern is that dedicating time to the arts reduces achievement in tested subjects. The study found no evidence of this trade-off." (Bowen)

**Policy implications.** A lead of one or two sentences says where the evidence gets its credibility, credits the authors, and carries the general caution. Then the items, each a bold imperative heading over a body of one to three sentences with an actor and a concrete fact or dollar figure where the paper gives one. Three numbered items by default (Bowen, Meer, Cortes); when the paper speaks to both practice and policy, items under For Practice and For Policy, as many as the paper supports (Kate's ruling, Oct 22). Credit the authors in the lead, and inside an item only for the paper's own interpretation. An imperative heading carries no "may": the hedge moves into the body ("The results suggest", "may consider", Cortes). Use "should" only where the paper does (Bowen's "should consider" is the paper's own).
- "Because schools were selected by lottery, this study provides credible evidence on the effects of expanding arts learning." (Bowen)
- "Do not expect a single nudge to change a major." (Meer)
- "Invest in depth, not just reach." (Holzman)

**Conclusions.** A short close: the gap turned with "yet", the study's claim, what is still open ("Questions remain about..."), and a plain takeaway. One paragraph or two, never three (Kate's ruling, Oct 22). Its last line may carry some energy, as a plain declarative sentence, never a question and never past the paper. No slogan, no echo of the title.
- "For schools serving students with disabilities, the evidence indicates that investing in arts education supports engagement, behavior, and learning." (Bowen)
- "What they often lack is the guidance to get there." (Holzman)

## 3. Numbers, hedging, rhythm, words

*Approved by Kate, Oct 22.*

**Numbers**
- Statistics and the sample size in digits, units in full: "percentage points", "standard deviations", never pp or SD; the % sign, never "percent". Small counts of things in words ("about five ... nearly ten", Bowen) **(Bowen's direction)**.
- Report numbers to the precision of the paper's table ("3.0 percentage points", not "3"). If the paper contradicts itself, use the table value and flag the conflict to Kate.
- Give each effect one piece of context: a baseline ("Previously, 9.7% to 13.9% of these students had received an infraction."), a concrete equivalent ("equivalent to about one additional day per school year"), or an outside benchmark ("a review of 357 studies of writing programs").
- Composition in parentheses ("learning disability (33%)"); a small share with its count ("only 13% (4 of 31)", Svajda-Hardy).
- No coefficients, standard errors, p-values or Ns in prose except the sample size. Significance in words: "did not differ significantly", "meets a less stringent standard of statistical significance".

**Hedging and causal language**
- Never firmer than the paper's own wording. The design licenses causal verbs for the main effects only: with a lottery or a cutoff, reduced, improved, raised, increased, leads to; with interviews, surveys, legal reviews or observational data, associated with, tied to, linked, reflect perceptions.
- Bowen names the design as the licence in three places: the summary, the overview ("This randomized controlled trial design allows differences in student outcomes to be attributed to expanded arts learning rather than pre-existing differences between schools.") and the policy lead.
- Mechanisms and recommendations are the authors': "The authors suggest ... may", "The authors note", "The authors view". Subgroups get "appeared" and "should be considered suggestive".
- The general caution once in the overview and once in the policy lead, never repeated at each finding (Kate's ruling, Oct 22). A caveat tied to one number or finding stays beside it ("The authors note this may represent an upper bound.", Bowen).
- Claims that went past the paper, to refuse: Meer calls "robust" a result the NBER working paper calls suggestive (the journal version the brief cites was not read); the Holzman paper says it "appears to be the first causal study", and the brief says "the most rigorous evidence to date"; Cortes's caveat says the patterns are descriptive, yet a finding says distance "drives" them.

**Rhythm**
- Mostly medium sentences, often two clauses joined by "so" or "as" to show cause: "Principals volunteered and were committed to the initiative's goals, so results may not generalize to schools without supportive leadership." Short sentences for headlines and turns, never for drama.
- No em dashes (Bowen and Meer have none; Cortes has four and Holzman two, which a draft does not copy). An aside with commas inside goes in parentheses.

**Words we use.** the group's full name every time, never an acronym ("students with disabilities"); plain names for the study groups ("initiative schools" and "comparison schools", Bowen); the study, the authors, researchers, this brief; plain verbs for what researchers did (paired, randomly selected, interviewed, reviewed, coded).

**Words we avoid.** we; acronyms the reader has to carry (SWDs, ITT, PSTs, URM, CBM); treatment and control; intent-to-treat, heterogeneity, spillover, bandwidth, underpowered, operationalize, homogeneity; praise words (substantial, sizable, striking, powerful, critical); "The majority of" (write Most); "Approximately" (write About); "typically developing" (write "students without disabilities"); figures of speech in body prose ("come at the expense of", "a sound investment", "open a door"); lead-ins ("The findings suggest that", "The authors argue that", "The following points are based on the data.").

## 4. From the paper to the brief

*Approved by Kate, Oct 22.*

The paper side is paraphrased, with its own key term in quotes.

1. **Make people the subject.** Paper: the share of SWDs with an infraction fell by 3 percentage points. Brief: "Students with disabilities in initiative schools were 3.0 percentage points less likely to receive a disciplinary infraction during the school year (Table 1)."
2. **Rename the study groups.** Paper: the "treatment group" attended more. Brief: "Students with disabilities in initiative schools had a daily attendance rate 0.5 percentage points higher than those in comparison schools, equal to about one additional day per school year."
3. **Drop the praise, keep the number, add context.** Paper: a "substantial" writing effect of 0.24 SDs. Brief: "Students with disabilities scored 0.24 standard deviations higher on the state writing test." Then the benchmark, and the significance caveat the praise word had hidden.
4. **Answer the worry, where the paper found no evidence.** Paper: no "spillover" to other subjects. Brief: "A common concern is that dedicating time to the arts reduces achievement in tested subjects. The study found no evidence of this trade-off."
5. **Credit the mechanism and keep the hedge.** Paper: "It is likely" the arts give an outlet for emotions. Brief: "The authors suggest that arts learning may help students manage complex emotions, supporting theories that it fosters self-control."
6. **Shorten, and move the condition to the end.** Paper: special education funding could be "one potential mechanism", when educationally relevant and aligned. Brief: "The authors note that special education funding could be one way to support arts programming when it aligns with students' educational needs."
7. **Define a term where it first carries weight.** Cortes defines the paper's "community college desert" in plain terms where it first appears, with the coined term in quotes.
8. **Swap the technical word and keep the hedge.** Paper: may have led to "homogeneity". Brief: "which may have resulted in similar perspectives."
9. **Say what the researchers did, not the method's name.** Paper: a "multistep qualitative research design". Brief: "Identifying relevant statutes required a systematic filtering process." (Svajda-Hardy). Paper: "underpowered". Brief: "The estimates are noisy because these outcomes are rare, so the study cannot rule out modest effects." (Meer)
10. **Turn a general implication into an action.** "Do not expect a single nudge to change a major." (Meer); "2. Offer transportation support for students in college deserts." (Cortes), with the hedge in the body.
11. **Paraphrase quotes.** Paper: a mentor teacher quoted with her years of experience. Brief: "a middle school mentor teacher noted that coursework in special education does not substitute for field experiences with students with disabilities."

**Moves to refuse, all found in published briefs:** calling a 2020 national survey "a recent review" and dropping its source (Nagro, Highlight 01); dropping the paper's "to some extent" for one group (Cortes); turning the authors' doubt into support (Meer's belonging line); a statistic the paper does not have ("1 in 4", Holzman; "85 school districts and 406 campuses", Cortes, where 406 appears in the paper only as a standard error); a subject slip that changes the claim ("higher education is the primary pathway", Cortes); cutting a non-causal caveat from a descriptive study and then generalizing from 75 responses ("demonstrates", Kwok, the author's edit).

## 5. What gets changed in a draft

*Approved by Kate, Oct 22.*

Kate's own edits, which a drafting tool should make before she has to. Authors' edits are marked as theirs.

1. **Cut the lead-in that announces a claim.** Draft: "The findings suggest that spending HB2 money on..." / Published: "Directing HB2 funds toward..." (Williams, editor not recorded). Also cut: "The following points are based on the data." and "The findings revealed a consistent message."
2. **Cut restatements the reader does not need.** Draft: "...0.24 standard deviations higher on the state writing test, nearly a quarter of a standard deviation." / Published: the sentence ends at "test" (Bowen). (Kwok's "new teachers, those at the start of their careers" to "new teachers" was the author's.)
3. **Cut framing around the reader's decision, and idioms.** Draft: "For under-resourced schools weighing whether they can make time for the arts, this result suggests that expanding arts learning does not have to come at the expense of core academics." / Published: "For under-resourced schools, these results suggest that expanding arts learning does not require sacrificing core academics." (Bowen)
4. **Lower the jargon.** "homogeneity of perspectives" to "similar perspectives"; "typically developing children" to "students without disabilities"; "reducing the statistical power to detect modest effects" to "so those effects are less certain" (Meer); "Approximately" to "About" everywhere (Svajda-Hardy).
5. **Fold the design gloss into the term.** Draft: "This design, called a randomized controlled trial, means that differences in student outcomes can be credited to the added arts learning rather than to differences between schools." / Published: "This randomized controlled trial design allows differences in student outcomes to be attributed to expanded arts learning rather than pre-existing differences between schools." (Bowen)
6. **Keep three highlights, and cut what the study cannot carry.** Meer, Kwok and Svajda-Hardy each went from four highlights to three. Bowen's fourth implication ("Look to sustained programs, such as teaching-artist residencies, as a promising model.") rested on something the study could not isolate, so it went. Holzman went from six implications to four.
7. **Write headlines as full sentences.** "EMERGE changed students' college trajectories without changing their test scores." (Holzman); "Writing scores rose." (Bowen)
8. **Cut method backstory down to the concrete limits, but keep the data-collection mode.** Svajda-Hardy's four limitation sentences became one: "Note that this study focuses on what state laws require on paper, not whether training is actually implemented or whether it improves outcomes."
9. **Titles: hook plus conventional subtitle, no question.** "Who Prepares Teachers to Manage a Classroom?" / "Managing Without Mandate"; "How Do New Teachers Bring Fairness Into the Classroom?" / "How New Teachers Bring Fairness Into the Classroom"; "What Expanded Arts Learning Did for Students With Disabilities" / "The Impact of Arts Learning on Students With Disabilities".
10. **End plain.** Draft: "the evidence suggests that making room for the arts is a sound investment in engagement, behavior, and learning." / Published: "the evidence indicates that investing in arts education supports engagement, behavior, and learning." (Bowen; Kwok's aspirational closing line was cut by the author.)
11. **Keep the builder's voice out of the brief.** "My recommendation:", "This brief earns **two tables**", "(verify during build)" and "Optional figure (probably skip)" never belong in brief text.
12. **Reread after every cut.** Trims left "serve students a broader range" and "Researchers identified those that" (Kwok, the author's trims) and "Developing and implementing the Individualized Education Program (IEP) concerns the legal document..." (Nagro).

**Edits that made it into print but are not to copy:** Svajda-Hardy's Highlight 03 "frequently contradict" where its Finding 3 says "in some cases"; Holzman's last-pass cut of "likely" ("gains came from advising, not academic preparation"); Kwok's "demonstrates"; Williams's "Most were near the Texas-Mexico border", "None were in central Texas" and "most lacked counselors" (the paper: most lacked a full-time nurse, social worker or psychologist, and many lacked a counselor); Meer's "did not cluster near the cutoff"; Holzman's "Target schools where the college-going culture is weakest." which dropped the paper's caution about a result found after the fact.

## 6. The models, and what each gives

- **Bowen:** the neutral body; every number with its context; the design named as the licence; the hedge set by the paper.
- **Meer:** the flat, even highlights, the null stated as plainly as the positives; the reader's picture of the intervention ("They knew only their own letter grade."); imperatives with a "Do not expect".
- **Cortes:** the hook title over a short place claim; each finding's arc from all students to the groups who make up the loss; the plain definition of the coined term; the "Still," that restores confidence after the limits.
- **Holzman:** the reader's question as a title; the program described concretely; short punchy lines in the highlights and the close ("What they often lack is the guidance to get there.").

**Kept out even from the models:** Cortes's praise words ("powerful and often overlooked", "striking") and its causal "drives" on descriptive patterns; Cortes's "406 campuses" and Holzman's "1 in 4", numbers the papers do not have; Holzman's cut of "likely"; Meer's "robust" and its generic close ("nudges serve as an initial step rather than a comprehensive solution").

**Nagro, the counter-example:** one caveat said four times; sentences as long as the paper's; a 2020 survey called "a recent review" with its source dropped; "twelve" next to "12".

## 7. For Kate to rule on

**Against STYLE-GUIDE.md**
1. **Anchors.** The guide's exemplars are Cortes (one of the four models) and the Clinical Teaching / Kwok brief (De La Cruz, Kwok & Kwok, 2024), written in "we" with PSTs. Proposal: the four models as anchors, new Grammarly samples from Bowen and Meer.
2. **Closing note.** Settled (Kate, Oct 22): the close may carry some energy, as the guide says, in a plain declarative line.
3. **"We".** The guide says Use "We found that…". No published brief uses "we" for its findings. Proposal: "The study found".
4. **Highlight count.** The guide allows 3 to 4; every brief since June has three.
5. **Energy.** Settled (Kate, Oct 22): the highlights and the close may carry some, the body none.
6. **Length.** The guide defaults to the fuller treatment, cuts with her sign-off. Proposal: keep the full interpretation, no restatements, no lists a table already shows.
7. **Design names.** The guide glosses "a cutoff-based research design"; Kate's Meer edit wrote "This regression discontinuity design"; Bowen describes, then names. One rule is needed.
8. **"Significant."** Kate's Bowen pass added "Engagement is a significant challenge" and "made a significant difference", while Bowen's writing result meets only a looser threshold. Proposal: the word only for statistics.
9. **Numbers in words.** The guide: spell out "where it aids readability". Proposal: section 3's rule.
10. **Title formula.** The guide wants a punchy title plus a research question; Kate turned the questions into statements.
11. **grammarly-voice-setup.md.** It models "about 40 percent", against the % rule; its Scholar (Formal) preset may be what lifts "rose" to "increased"; Sample 1 carries Cortes's "85 school districts and 406 campuses"; Sample 3 uses PSTs.

**Open between the newest briefs**
12. **Register.** Bowen's pass lifted everyday words ("rose" to "increased", "set aside" to "allocated", "Before it began" to "Prior to its launch"), possibly Grammarly's Scholar preset, yet Bowen kept "Writing scores rose." and Holzman and Meer stay plain. Which way?
13. **Question titles.** Meer and Holzman, two of the four models, ask a reader's question ("Can a Single Email Shape What Students Study?"); section 2 says no question, from your edits to Kwok and Svajda-Hardy. Allow a reader's question?
